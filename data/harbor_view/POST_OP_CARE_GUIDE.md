# POST-OPERATIVE CARE & HOME RECOVERY GUIDE

**Harbor View Veterinary Clinic** — *Compassionate Medicine. Modern Care. Lifelong Companionship.*

Thank you for trusting us with your companion. Going home after surgery is a big step, and this guide walks you through every phase of recovery. It covers spay/neuter, soft tissue mass excision, and dental extractions. Questions are always welcome. Call us at **(555) 438-2323**.

## 1. Incision Site Monitoring & Protection

- **Elizabethan Collar (E-Collar) Compliance:** The E-collar must remain securely fastened **24/7 for 10 to 14 days**. Even 30 seconds of licking can introduce oral bacteria or cause suture dehiscence (the incision opening). You should be able to fit two fingers between the collar and your pet's neck. It should be snug enough that it cannot slip over the head.
  - Pets may bump into furniture for the first day or two. Move low objects out of the way, and raise food and water bowls.
  - Inflatable or soft collars are acceptable only if your veterinarian has approved them, and only if your pet cannot reach the incision.
- **Incision Evaluation Checklist:** Inspect the surgical site **twice daily** under good lighting, and take a photo each day so you can spot change.
  - *Normal Healing:* Mild redness, slight firm swelling along the suture line, clean and closed margins, and a small amount of bruising (especially in light-skinned pets).
  - *Abnormal Symptoms (Call the Clinic Immediately):* Active dripping of fresh blood, thick yellow or green discharge, foul odor, a gap in the tissue margins, visible suture material or tissue protruding, a swelling that grows larger, or extreme heat.
- **Bathing Prohibition:** *No bathing, swimming, or outdoor moisture exposure for 14 days.* Moisture carries bacteria into the sub-dermal suture tracks. Do not apply creams, peroxide, or alcohol unless a veterinarian instructs you to.
- **Suture Removal / Recheck:** If your pet has skin sutures or staples, they are removed at a recheck visit at **day 10 to 14**. Many of our closures are buried and dissolve on their own, but the recheck still applies.

## 2. Temperature & Systemic Warning Signs

Normal rectal temperature for dogs and cats is **100.5°F–102.5°F (38.1°C–39.2°C)**.

| Reading | Meaning | Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Below 100.0°F (37.8°C) | Possible hypothermia or shock | Warm gently and call us the same day |
| 100.5–102.5°F (38.1–39.2°C) | Normal | Continue home care |
| 103.0–103.9°F (39.4–39.9°C) | Mild fever, possible infection | Call us the same day |
| 104.0°F (40.0°C) or higher | Significant fever | Call immediately and come in |

Also call us for vomiting more than twice, refusal of food beyond 24 hours after the first evening, lethargy, pale gums, or a swollen abdomen.

## 3. Post-Surgical Activity Restriction

- Strict confinement to a small, carpeted room or crate without stairs, couches, or jumping obstacles.
- Dogs must be taken outdoors strictly on a **short 6-foot leash** for elimination only. No running, roughhousing, or dog park visits for 14 days.
- Cats stay indoors in a quiet room with a low-sided litter box. Use a non-clumping or paper litter for the first 7 days after surgery.
- Check the incision after every trip outdoors.

| Recovery Day | Expected Status | Activity |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Days 1–3 | Groggy, reduced appetite, mild soreness | Rest only, leash for elimination |
| Days 4–7 | Brighter, appetite returns | Rest only; E-collar stays on |
| Days 8–10 | Feels well, so risk of overdoing it is highest | Restrict firmly, and the E-collar stays on |
| Days 10–14 | Incision closed, recheck visit | Gradual return to normal after the vet clears your pet |

## 4. Multimodal Pain Medication Schedule

Administer prescribed medications strictly with food to avoid gastric ulceration. The doses below are standard references. **Your pet's discharge sheet is the authority for your pet's actual dose.**

- **Carprofen (NSAID, dogs only):** 2.2 mg/kg orally every 12 hours. *Do not combine with human pain relievers (aspirin, acetaminophen/Tylenol, or ibuprofen are dangerous or lethal to pets).* Stop and call us if you see vomiting, black stool, or loss of appetite.
- **Gabapentin (Neuropathic / Sedative):** 10 mg/kg orally every 8–12 hours for mild sedation and nerve pain.
- **Cats:** Cats do not receive carprofen. Pain control for cats is prescribed individually, for example buprenorphine 0.02 mg/kg transmucosally every 8–12 hours, as directed on the discharge sheet.
- **Antibiotics:** Prescribed only when clinically indicated. If they are, finish the entire course even if the incision looks perfect.

**Sample 12-Hour Dosing Schedule**

| Time | Task |
| :--- | :--- |
| 7:00 AM | Small meal, then carprofen and gabapentin |
| 12:00 PM | Incision check and a short leash walk |
| 3:00 PM | Gabapentin (if every-8-hour dosing) |
| 7:00 PM | Small meal, then carprofen and gabapentin |
| 9:00 PM | Final incision check and photo, and E-collar check |

**Missed dose:** If the next dose is less than half the interval away, skip it. Never double up.

## 5. Feeding & Elimination

- Offer a small meal (about a quarter to half of the usual portion) the evening of surgery.
- Normal appetite and bowel movements should return within 24 to 48 hours. Call us if your pet has not passed stool within 72 hours.

## 6. When to Call or Go to an Emergency Hospital

Call **(555) 438-2323** during clinic hours. If we are closed, go to the **Regional Animal Specialty & Emergency Center (24/7 ER), (555) 948-3700**, for bleeding that will not stop, collapse, difficulty breathing, or pale or white gums.
